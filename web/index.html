Reading the synoptic overlay
Isobars: lines of equal sea-level pressure every 4 hPa; the label is the last two digits (16 = 1016 hPa, 96 = 996). Closer lines mean stronger wind. Wind blows nearly along them, anticlockwise around a low and clockwise around a high, angled slightly in toward the low.
H / L: high and low pressure centres with their central pressure. Highs usually bring settled, drier weather (and subsidence that can dry fuels); lows bring cloud, wind and precipitation.
Cold front: colder air pushing in; triangles point the way it's moving. Most important for fire: warm, dry, gusty (often SW) winds ahead of it, then an abrupt wind shift (often to NW) with strong gusts as it passes, which can turn a flank into a head fire. Temperature drops and RH rises behind it.
Warm front: warmer air advancing; half-circles point the way it's moving. Gradual: thickening cloud and often rain or snow ahead, then a gentler wind turn (e.g. SE to SW). Usually less dangerous than a cold front, but the warm, drier air behind it can raise fire danger.
Stationary front: a boundary that isn't moving much. Neither air mass is pushing, so the symbols face each other from opposite sides. Winds on each side tend to blow along it; it can sit for a day or more or waver back and forth, often with lingering cloud and rain. Watch for it to start moving again as a cold or warm front.
Occluded front: where a cold front has caught up with the warm front ahead of it near the low, lifting the warm air off the ground. Both symbols on the same side, pointing the way it's moving. Marks a mature or weakening low; brings a wind shift, cloud and precipitation as it passes.
Trough: an elongated area of lower pressure without a sharp air-mass change. Expect a wind shift along it and sometimes showers or thunderstorms; a trough ahead of a cold front can bring the wind shift early.
Triple point: where cold, warm (or stationary) and occluded fronts meet, often a focus for new storm development and sharp wind changes.

Isobars and H/L come from the RDPS model for the hour shown; fronts are drawn by NOAA's Weather Prediction Center and appear only within 3 h of their analysis or forecast times (see the legend). For a base, the front watch in the point panel gives distance, speed and the forecast wind shift.
